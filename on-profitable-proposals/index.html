Stand Out Proposals

As a consultant within a fast-growing department, I'm always looking for ways for my company to get our foot in the door with a new client. Most of the high-revenue generating projects within my department can be attributed to roadway and bridge design projects as well as civil site development (not in my wheelhouse!). These projects bring in a lot of money but they can be the hardest to acquire.

American Indian Tribes are sovereign nations and as such are entitled to choose who they contract with to perform work. In fact, tribes are even exempt from Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, which prohibits discrimination in employment based on race or national origin. Essentially, tribal governments are allowed to apply "Indian Preference" when choosing a contractor. If a company is owned by Indians, they can receive varying degrees of preference in the contract awarding process --- as defined in each tribe's procurement standards.

Since roadway/bridge design projects and other engineering services are so expensive to tribes, they constitute a financial risk. One service that my company offers is Long Range Transportation Plan (LRTP) development. It's a cheap way for us to show off our work ethic, get to know the transportation staff personally and serve as a launchpad for other, more profitable ventures with the tribe. This is the primary focus of my work, and consequently I'm also the one putting together proposals for potential clients.

My employer has no enrolled Indians in our ownership body. How are we to compete with Indian-owned AEC firms? What can I, a mere geographer, do to increase our chances of being selected for a project? This is where the featured image of this post comes into play. 

Proposal requirements are usually pretty cut and dry, with not much room for creativity. While our team is seasoned and fully capable of delivering high-quality LRTPs, it's hard to convey that through walls of text detailing our prior experiences in this space. One thing I try to do when preparing a proposal is to make the cover page eye-catching and truly unique. I do this by making a custom map.

In this case, our potential client was the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe. Their reservation is bisected by the border between North and South Dakota, which I deliberately did not call attention to in this map. By leaving out this arbitrary imaginary line, created as a product of manifest destiny, our map communicates a subtle acknowledgement of their tribal sovereignty. 

One thing I also do whenever I am making a map for a client (whether it's for a proposal or not) is do some research on their history. One detail I discovered while researching this tribe is their special connection to the Missouri River, which forms the eastern boundary of their territory. Rather than having the red line territorial boundary run over the satellite imagery of the river, I instead chose to remove the eastern boundary line all together and insert a dark blue polygon of the river, which makes it stand out from the rest of the imagery. This decision is another subtle acknowledgement that the tribe's connection with the river is not just as a mere dividing line, but an integral part of the tribe's territory. 

I used some of my favorite font combinations on this map, which I discovered through trial and error. I always find that darkening the surrounding area and adding a subtle hillshade effect always produces a nice result.

Of course, a visually appealing map alone will not win any contracts, but it shows the reviewer before they even open the packet that we can make high-quality work that is tailored to the individual tribal client. 

We just submitted this proposal 2 days ago at the time of writing, I'll report back if we win the contract.
