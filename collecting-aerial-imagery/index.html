Collecting Aerial Imagery

Flying a drone is scary. I do it all the time.

When doing site visits, whether it is to meet with the client or to perform field work, one thing that always travels with me is the department drone. We have access to a Skydio 2+, possibly the most fool-proof drone on the market. It's a far cry from the drones I learned to fly at the University of Louisville where the pilot was required to take corrective action to keep the thing stable at nearly all times. Thank goodness I learned what I did then and got my FAA Part 107 Certification before I got too lazy on the Skydio! The Skydio 2+ takes off and lands nearly autonomously, has object detection that prevents the drone from colliding with tree branches and telephone wire, and has nearly lossless video streaming back to the controller. It's a real treat to fly.

Well, it would be, if it weren't for everything else. 

Unfortunately for me, the conditions I am required to fly our Skydio are often not ideal. Usually I am taking profile photos of bridges over creeks, rivers and bayous. Even with collision avoidance technology, getting a drone far enough away from a bridge to get a good shot is like threading a needle. Tree branches love to stretch out over narrow bodies of water from both banks, creating a kind of webbed matrix of woody sinew. Get too close to a branch and the drone will violently recoil in the air--- so hard that it might push itself dangerously close to a branch reaching from the other side of the water body, triggering a dangerous snowball effect of violent thrashing from the drone as it attempts to remove itself from the situation. 

Another hazard of flying low to the ground over undeveloped terrain is avian threats. Yes, you read that correctly. I can't tell you how many close calls I've had resulting from near suicidal barrages of small birds attempting to attack our drone. Fortunately I have not injured one of these brave warriors yet, but it is always something to be vigilant for. 

Finally, takeoff and landing can make your blood pressure spike in certain conditions. There are often times where the bridge I need to snap a photo of are part of a major arterial road or even an interstate. These kinds of roads usually don't leave much room in the right-of-way for cars to pull over, much less to take off and land a drone. Many times, this procedure has to be a carefully coordinated affair. One thing you never want to do is take off a drone where drivers can see it, lest they get distracted and cause a collision. When a gap in traffic presents itself, the drone needs to be launched from the roadside quickly and gain as much altitude as possible before another vehicle comes along to potentially see it. The drone must traverse the narrow right-of-way clearing between roadway and tree line, careful to account for the massive wind gusts created by tractor trailers barreling down the road. These tractor trailers also cause an instinctual fear response when they barrel past the pilot (me!) at highway speeds--- it's something that I have to consciously suppress. 

In the end though, the cool-factor of flying drones as part of your work outweighs the stress induced by the operation of said drones. Sometimes you get to capture interesting spots that may be used by engineers to develop on. The picture featured in this post is being used to help develop a revenue-generating site for the Coushatta Tribe in Louisiana. Eventually there will be a roundabout here that facilitates easy in-and-out access to a travel center, which should be a great boon to the tribe. Further north in the state, near the Jena Band of Choctaw Indians headquarters, I snapped the cover photo for this website. Depicting a bridge on US84 over Little River.
